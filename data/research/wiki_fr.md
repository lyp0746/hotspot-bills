

# ===== 2026 European unrest =====
In the latter half of 2026, a period of unrest has gripped large parts of the European continent. Demonstrations have occurred in France, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Albania, Belgium, Germany, Austria, and Ireland. Though largely unconnected, these events usually have occurred against the backdrop of affordability crises and calls for better living and working conditions.


== Background ==

The protests have unfolded in the face of rising unaffordability, poor working and educational conditions, and dissatisfaction with ruling governments. Several countries are facing mounting pressure to reign in their deficits while higher borrowing costs make debts more difficult to service. Austria is running a budget in 2026 above 4% of their GDP, while France and Belgium are running ones 5% above. 


== Demonstrations ==


=== Albania ===

Protests erupted in Albania in late May 2026 in response to the planned development of a new resort on Sazan Island, an ecologically protected island. The development, which was to be undertaken by Jared Kushner, prompted major backlash, ushering in what became known as the Flamingo Revolution. Initially confined to concerns over the Sazan resort, the demonstrations grew to call for the resignation of incumbent prime minister Edi Rama, the Socialist politician who has been running the country since 2013. Throughout his thirteen years in power, Rama has faced criticism for alleged corruption, misuse of public funds, and favouritism.
A fresh wave of demonstrations erupted on 13 October, when students began protesting over higher educational costs, a lack of financial aid, conditions at universities, and future job prospects. Protestors clashed with the riot police.


=== Austria ===
Student groups are preparing for fresh demonstrations following an announcement by the national university association of €1 billion cuts for the next three-year budgetary period.


=== Belgium ===
Demonstrators convened in Brussels and Liège on 17 October in response to the deteriorating conditions of schools and the increasing costs of education. In addition, the Belgian police have launched tear gas and water cannons after accusing the protestors of igniting fires and launching missiles. Up to 195 people have been arrested in Belgium for the protests, with 5 officers sustaining major injuries, with some individuals relocating to construct barricades. In response to the protests, the Interior Minister of Belgium, Bernard Quintin, stated that “what is unfolding in Brussels is no longer mere protest and all necessary means will be used to restore order in the face of the rioters.”, 
In Liège, there was also a protest in response to the increasing costs of education and the difficulty in obtaining study materials.


=== France ===

Demonstrations regarding schools in France began on 18 September, when teachers in Créteil, a suburb of Paris, began going on strike over staffing shortages. Students later aided them in solidarity, spiraling into a nationwide protest movement. The demonstrations were initially limited to Paris, but gradually grew to encompass the rest of the countries and absorb broader dissatisfaction over the French economy, particularly regarding fuel prices. Due to the rising fuel prices and cost-of-living crisis, the Yellow vests movement saw a revitalisation on social media in September, scheduling a mass demonstration for 17 October. 
On 17 October, fuel protesters and workers mobilised and joined together with student protesters, escalating the unrest. Many monuments were vandalised and damaged, including the Monument à la République in Paris and Hôtel de Ville in Limoges which was set on fire, listed by the French government as a Monument historique. The protests have seen nearly a million people take part, with widespread rioting, looting, and destruction taking place across France. According to the BBC, 1,500 shops and businesses were ransacked or destroyed during the riots on 17 October.


=== Germany ===

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz of the centre-right CDU, who took office in May 2025, saw his and his party's popularity steeply declining since April 2026. Elections in September in the states of Saxony-Anhalt and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern as well as public opinion polling for the 2029 federal election saw the right-extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) gain unprecedented traction, while The Left won the Berlin election in the same month. Merz's ambition to enforce a disputed reform package by the end of 2026 led to a rift with ruling "Grand Coalition” partner SPD and resulted in nationwide protests, albeit of mostly peaceful nature.
The school strikes are supported by student councils and unions, trade unions, peace organisations, churches and various groups of the political left against conscription and militarization through demonstrations and school strikes.


=== Greece ===
On 13 October, Greek students began protests in the Syntagma Square of Athens, which are centered around the issues of unaffordable prices of living for students, mainly in terms of student housing. Additionally protestors brought up complaints of the lack of funding for the public education system. Demonstrators erected tents outside of the Ministry of National Economy and Finance and the Hellenic Parliament, mainly as a blockade, but according to the Hellenic Broadcasting Corporation the tents are also a comment about the condition of housing in Greece. The organizers said that the tents were planned to stand for the following two days, and then a protest concert would be held the following week on 20 October.


=== Ireland ===
On 1 October, more than 1,500 students marched in Dublin to protest the effects of cost of living and student fees. The event was organised by the Aontas na Mac Léinn in Éirinn, the national students' union for Irish higher education.,


=== Italy ===

On 16 October, Italian students in major cities like Milan, Turin, Bologna, Verona, Rome, and Bari started protesting, inspired by their French counterparts. The mobilisation was denominated No Meloni Day, referring to Giorgia Meloni, the Prime Minister of Italy. Demonstrators protested against the conditions of Italian educational facilities, against the measures adopted by the government to limit the number of foreign students in classrooms,  and demanded that the government start spending more on education and less on weapons. 
In Rome, demonstrators threw firecrackers and cherry bombs in front of the Ministry of Education, calling for Giuseppe Valditara, the Italian Minister of Education, and asking him to “get out of the building”. During the second day of the protest, 17 October, in Milan, Turin, and Bologna, riots erupted from the protests, that had become about more than issues of the students, but general social issues. In Milan especially the riot took a toll on the city, and at the end of the day, many shop windows and other public properties had been severely damaged. 
The students' protest became part of a broader climate of social unrest. This also saw road haulers and farmers protesting and going on strike because of the high cost of diesel fuel and the rising of the already high cost of living. Severe fuel shortages were reported in Southern Italy, alongside empty shelves in supermarkets. In Apulia students, road haulers and farmers demonstrated together. Minister of Infrastructure and Transport Matteo Salvini scheduled a meeting with representatives of road haulers for 20 October in Milan. Representatives of Calabrian road haulers have said that they won't stop striking and demonstrating if the confrontation with the government does not result in a satisfying outcome.

# ===== 2026 French fuel protests =====
On 15 September 2026, protests driven by rising fuel prices erupted in France. The protests saw a revival of the 2018 Yellow vests movement. A mobilisation was held by protesters on 17 October and was joined by student protesters and others in nationwide demonstrations, which descended into the 17 October riots in Paris and other cities.


== Background ==
Due to the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz in the 2026 Iran war and escalation of the Yemeni civil war, the price of diesel in France had rose to €2.37 per liter ($2.72 per liter), just 1c below its record high of €2.38.
The yellow vest movement began in 2018 as a protest against fuel taxes and the rising cost of living, it has since returned as a symbol for the 2026 protests. Former members took part in the 17 October demonstrations against living costs. The demonstrations lacked a single organizational leadership, yet they gave voice to diverse forms of social discontent, with grievances including cost of living, education, public spending, social inequality, and political corruption.


== Protests ==


=== Beginning ===
Fishermen, police officers, farmers, construction workers, energy workers, medical workers, university staff and other groups participated in protests. On 17 September, fishermen took their boats and blockaded two ports in Nice and Sète, obstructing maritime traffic. Others burned tires and wooden pallets on roads to petrol depots in Frontignan, preventing access.


=== Yellow vests protests ===


==== 17 October: "Act I" ====

Throughout September, a resurgence of the Yellow vests movement occurred on social media, with calls for nationwide demonstrations on 17 October. The movement quickly spread online. On 17 October, people across France took to the streets to protest the rising cost of living. The demonstrations coincided with massive student protests, bringing together a wide range of groups—former Yellow vest members, climate campaigners, trade unionists, high school students, and farmers. Around 110 cities saw demonstrations. Hundreds of actions and dozens of rallies were held nationwide, with officials estimating as many as 170,000 people joined, with tens of thousands estimated in Paris alone. 
In Paris, a huge crowd of demonstrators gathered around Place de la République and a riot occurred, vehicles were set on fire and shops were looted. Extinction Rebellion activists climbed the Monument à la République and placed a giant yellow vest over the statue of Marianne, bearing the words «1961, la police noie les Algérien‑nes / 2026, la police mutile les lycéen‑nes» ("1961, the police drown Algerians / 2026, the police mutilate high school students"), in reference to the 1961 Paris massacre which happened on the same day. The French Interior Ministry stated that 550,000 people took part in the demonstrations, while the CGT trade union and Yellow vests estimated the number of participants at 800,000. French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu also announced that 1,200 people had been arrested and 150 police officers had been injured. The combined protests marked an escalation of civil unrest in France, with the Hôtel de Ville in Limoges collapsing after being firebombed by rioters. According to the BBC, 1,500 shops and businesses ransacked or destroyed during the riots on 17 October.


==== 24 October: "Act II" ====
The Yellow vests announced "Act II" for 24 October. Their announcement made several appeals including the ability to live with dignity, right to assemble, condemnation of police brutality, and resignation of president Emmanuel Macron.


== Reactions ==
On 16 September, Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said that the government would increase its petrol subsidies for affected industries to the maximum authorized level allowed under EU rules until 31 December. The minor subsidies did not quell the protests, which resumed on 17 September. On 7 October, Lecornu addressed the protesters and announced a release of  10 million barrels from France's strategic diesel reserves to bring down fuel prices.
French president Emmanuel Macron announced an emergency G7 meeting. On 2 October, the G7 agreed to release 100 million reserve barrels of oil and diesel fuel over the next 4 months.
Place Publique presidential candidate Raphaël Glucksmann criticised the French government for its "lack of preparedness" over the cost-of-living crisis.
In response to the rioting on 17 October, Macron condemned the destruction and promised a "firm response" to the combined protesters. On 19 October, police issued curfews in Paris and other cities.

# ===== 2026 French student protests =====
Since 18 September 2026, a series of demonstrations and blockades by lycée students has been taking place in France and later, to a lesser degree, in Belgium, Albania, Greece, and Italy, having originated in Créteil, a commune in the suburbs of Paris. The protests had originally started in French Guiana, where students had first blocked a high school on 7 September to denounce degrading learning conditions.
On 6 October, the protests developed into a nationwide mobilisation involving students, teachers, parents, and trade unions. According to The Conversation, unions estimated that 450,000 people participated nationwide while the French government put the figure at 250,000. The movement is marked by blockades of high schools, gatherings, and demonstrations. As reasons for participating in the protests, students have cited teacher shortages, overcrowded classrooms, exhausting timetables, run-down buildings, and stress linked to the university application website Parcoursup as well as high temperatures in classrooms that reached up to 40 °C (104 °F) during the 2026 European heatwaves.
The initial police response was heavy-handed, prominently maiming several students with heavy-duty weaponry, and eventually leading to 6,800 arrests. By October, parts of the movement were engaged in violence against police and some management staff, especially headteachers. The French Interior Ministry reported that 1,949 people were arrested on 1 October. The French government has accused the far-left of stirring up the unrest for political gain. Protesting groups and parties, meanwhile, contended that the movement was spontaneous and accused the government and right-wing parties of engaging in conspiratorial thinking.
The police response, which led to more than 6,800 people being taken into custody, most of them minors, drew criticism from human rights organisations: Amnesty International denounced a "disproportionate" use of force, Human Rights Watch reported serious injuries to children, and the UN Human Rights Office expressed concern and called for de-escalation. The government rejected accusations of police repression, with Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez maintaining that the response had been proportionate in almost all cases and that some protests had turned into riots.


== Background ==


=== Study conditions and government budget ===
The protests came at the start of the school year and at the same time as a public-sector strike over the government's budget plans, which include €61 billion in new spending cuts. Students cited unfilled teaching posts, overcrowded classes, long timetables and deteriorating facilities among their grievances. A 17-year-old pupil at the Lycée Paul-Éluard in Saint-Denis described temporary toilets without water, rats and cockroaches in school, and temperatures ranging from −5 °C in winter to 40 °C in summer in temporary classrooms. Another pupil described rooms with too few tables, noting that teachers had explained their own discontent before the pupils decided to join them. Ryad Rani, president of the secondary-students' union Union Syndicale Lycéenne (USL), cited a pupil who had gone seven months without a French teacher in a year with a French exam, and classes of 32 to 36 students. Pupils elsewhere told ICI of classes of 35.
Explaining the protests, one sociologist told Al Jazeera that major concerns included inequality, cuts to public investment, democratic rights, and a desire to prioritize social welfare rather than militarization.
The Créteil rectorat told Agence France-Presse that demands still varied by school: teachers not replaced, class size, heavy timetables, shortages of education assistants, nurses, social workers and managers, incidents between pupils and teachers left without follow-up, and deficient undergraduate admissions web portal "Parcoursup". France Info summarised what it was hearing from pupils as more resources, smaller classes, buildings in better condition, enough teachers and staff, and less stress from Parcoursup.


=== Previous protests ===
In autumn 1990, lycée students, many from working-class Paris suburbs, protested over safety, cleanliness, staffing and materials. The protests reached on the order of 100,000 people in Paris and about 150,000 nationwide. Education minister Lionel Jospin answered with an emergency plan and an increase in the education budget.
In early 2005, lycée students and their teachers mobilised against reforms to the education system made by François Fillon, and in particular against a reform of the baccalauréat that they believed would lead to greater inequalities in the value of diplomas from different schools. Between 15,000 and 40,000 students took part in the protests. Fillon backed down and announced a government amendment removing changes to the baccalauréat from the bill.
In spring 2018, opposition to the ORE law and to the new Parcoursup platform was carried mainly by university occupations. Lycée blockades were scattered. In the autumn, lycée students mobilised again, against Parcoursup and against Jean-Michel Blanquer's baccalauréat reform. On 11 December, hundreds of establishments were blocked or badly disrupted. Le Monde diplomatique later wrote that this map could not be explained only by the knock-on effect of the Yellow vests protests, then at their height, but that the Yellow Vest movement was part of the context. Parcoursup was kept, with some adjustments, and the baccalauréat reform went ahead.


=== Emergence of the protests ===
The mobilisation began on 21 September at the Lycée Antoine-de-Saint-Exupéry in Créteil. The day after a teachers' strike on 17 September against lack of resources and staff, pupils there organised a protest. Pupils said they felt neglected by the Ministry of National Education and cited lack of resources and staff, overcrowded classes, and rooms short of tables. An academic source and the USL later presented that mobilisation as the start of the national movement.
Contemporaneous reports, including Le Monde and a 24 September account by actu.fr, dated the spreading movement to Monday 21 September at the same lycée, where several teaching posts were unfilled. The Créteil rectorate said on 24 September that demands still varied by school.
Through 25 September the spread stayed local. Actu.fr, citing the Streamer academy, said about 10 lycées were affected by 24 September, mainly in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne, and described a copycat effect on social networks. That morning, pupils tried to block the entrance of the Lycée Auguste-Blanqui in Saint-Ouen until late morning. An academy spokesperson told AFP there had been a gathering and material damage but no injuries. Police dispersed the crowd without incident.
On 25 September the Créteil rectorate said the movement was gaining momentum and counted "a good dozen" lycées blocked, above all in Seine-Saint-Denis and Val-de-Marne. It said some mobilisations had produced crowds and material damage. The Versailles rectorate reported disruption at three of 62 lycées in Essonne and mobilisations at five of 65 in Hauts-de-Seine. Le Monde later wrote that about 60 Paris-region lycées were disrupted on 24 and 25 September, that about 12 percent of the 468 public lycées saw attempted blockades, and that around 10 were actually blocked. Rani told AFP that it had started at Saint-Exupéry and "spread very quickly", and cited shortages of education assistants, nurses and social workers.


== Organization ==

The USL's national call, for 29 September, named four grievances: lack of teachers and staff, "dilapidated lycées", overloaded timetables and classes, and the stress caused by Parcoursup. Rani said classes sometimes held 32 to 35 pupils and asked protesters to remain peaceful. The union later called a second day on 1 October, an "act two", and a further day of action on 6 October. On RTL on 30 September, Rani said dialogue bodies run by the ministry had been "abandoned", and that it was for the minister to take the first step. Following their meeting with Education Minister Édouard Geffray on 2 October, student union leaders voiced their dissatisfaction with the government's response and called for the mobilisation to continue. The USL announced further demonstrations for 6 October, with several trade-union confederations, including the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), announcing that they would join the action. Other supporting trade union confederations included the Workers' Force (FO), National Union of Autonomous Trade Unions (UNSA), French Democratic Confederation of Labour (CFDT), Solidaires, French Confederation of Christian Workers (CFTC), and the Confédération Paysanne. The teachers' unions Unitary Trade Union Federation (FSU) and National Union of Independent Colleges and High Schools (SNALC) called for strikes and supported the protests as well.

# ===== 2027 French presidential election =====
Presidential elections are scheduled to be held in France on 18 April 2027, with a second round on 2 May if no candidate secures a majority vote. The election may be held earlier under exceptional circumstances if the presidency falls vacant before then.
Incumbent president Emmanuel Macron is ineligible to run, as the French constitution limits presidents to two consecutive terms.


== Background ==

The dissolution of the National Assembly announced by Emmanuel Macron following the 2024 European election triggered a period of political instability. Michel Barnier and François Bayrou were each ousted in confidence votes, in December 2024 and September 2025, and Sébastien Lecornu became prime minister. 
In March 2025, Marine Le Pen, a declared candidate for the National Rally, was found guilty of embezzlement of European Parliament funds and sentenced at first instance to five years of ineligibility with immediate effect, preventing her from running in upcoming elections. On 7 July 2026, the Court of Appeal of Paris upheld her conviction but reduced her ineligibility sentence to 45 months, 30 of them suspended, and ordered one year under electronic monitoring, clearing the way for her candidacy. Following the ruling, Le Pen said she would run and appeal to the Court of Cassation, which is expected to rule by early January 2027 at the latest.
The left-wing New Popular Front alliance formed in 2024 broke apart over strategic disagreements, particularly between the Socialist Party and La France Insoumise. A planned unitary primary was abandoned over the summer of 2026, and the Socialist Party, Place Publique and the Republican and Socialist Left instead held their own primary on 11 October, which Raphaël Glucksmann won in the first round. Jean-Luc Mélenchon (LFI) and Fabien Roussel (PCF) are running separately.
On the right, The Republicans' members voted on 19 April 2026 to back Bruno Retailleau directly rather than hold a primary; Laurent Wauquiez then withdrew his bid and David Lisnard left the party, while Xavier Bertrand plans to run as an independent. In the centre, Édouard Philippe (Horizons) and Gabriel Attal (Renaissance) are both running, amid fears that their rivalry could leave the two run-off places to the far right and far left.
The number of declared or presumed candidates is unprecedented in the Fifth Republic, with projections of 17 or more candidates against the record of 16 set in 2002. Political scientist Bruno Jeanbart attributes this to "the fragmentation of the political landscape and the decline of the party system" that began in 2017. Political scientists Antoine Bristielle and Élie Michel see it as a symptom of a deep crisis of representation, with parties struggling to fulfill their role in selecting candidates and personal ambitions breaking free from the party framework.


== Electoral system ==
The president of the French Republic is elected to a five-year term under the two-round system as stipulated in Article 7 of the French constitution. If no candidate secures an absolute majority (50% + 1) of votes in the first round, a second round is held two weeks later between the two candidates who received the most votes. Per the constitution, the first round of the presidential election must be held between 20 and 35 days before the conclusion of the president's current five-year term. Emmanuel Macron's second term, which began on 14 May 2022, is scheduled to end on 13 May 2027, meaning that the first round of the presidential election is scheduled to be held between 8 and 23 April 2027.
To be listed on the first-round ballot, candidates must secure 500 signatures (often referred to as parrainages) from national or local elected officials from at least 30 different departments or overseas collectivities, with no more than a tenth of these signatories from any single department. According to the Article 6 of the French constitution, the president cannot "exercise more than two consecutive periods in office".

# ===== 2026 French municipal elections =====
The 2026 French municipal elections were held on 15 and 22 March 2026 to elect the municipal councils of the approximately 35,000 communes in France.


== Background ==

During the last municipal election in 2020, which was marked by the COVID-19 pandemic and the highest abstention rate for local elections in French history, the left reversed the trend of 2014. They narrowed the vote gap with the right by flipping cities such as Annecy, Bordeaux, Marseille, and Saint-Paul. Nevertheless, the right managed to reclaim several cities from the left, including Metz. The National Rally strengthened its local presence, winning the city of Perpignan while holding all the municipalities it controlled.
The 2022 French presidential election and legislative election saw the power of President of France Emmanuel Macron and his presidential majority weakened by the rise of both the far-right and the left. Concurrently, The Republicans were further diminished, resulting in their smallest parliamentary group since the start of the Fifth Republic.
In the European Parliament election of June 9, 2024, the National Rally saw its support soar, while the presidential majority list lost nearly half it seats. The left entered the election divided, despite having previously united in 2022 under NUPES. The joint list of the Socialist Party and Place Publique, as well as La France Insoumise, gained seats, while the French Communist Party again failed to secure representation, and The Ecologists plummeted from 13 seats to just five.
On the evening of the results of the European election, Emmanuel Macron dissolved the National Assembly. New legislative elections were held on June 30 and July 7, 2024, further upending an already disrupted political landscape. The left reunited and formed the New Popular Front in just a few hours. The Republicans faced an unprecedented crisis when their president, Éric Ciotti, formed an alliance with the National Rally. Though the centrists, headed by Renaissance, struggled with the president's decision to dissolve parliament, they maintained their Ensemble pour la République coalition.
Between the second round of voting, faced with the surge of the far-right, the left called for a "Republican Front", which most qualifying candidates joined. The final results saw the left-wing alliance NFP win a narrow plurality of 193 deputies. The National Rally became the largest single-party group in the National Assembly with 125 deputies, while Éric Ciotti formed his own group with 18 candidates. The center suffered a major collapse, losing one-thirds of its representatives.
Many mayors have resigned since 2020.


=== Campaign ===
The race in Paris was considered to be important for the 2027 French presidential election, in a fierce battle between the left and right. The election in Nice was also competitive between centre-right and far-right. Overall, the left was successful in cities such as Paris, Marseille, and Lyon, while the right was successful in cities including Nice and Toulouse.


== Results ==
26,182,365 valid votes were cast in the first round, and 9,391,407 valid votes were cast in the second round.
